Week 2 Reading Companion

Alfaro, Gilpin & Ruggie — three readings, three genres, three approaches

Read the articles in this order

Alfaro → Gilpin → Ruggie. Remember that Ruggie is due on Wednesday, while you should have completed Alfaro and Gilpin by Monday.

Alfaro establishes what we actually know about whether, when, and why FDI helps host countries. Gilpin then shows you that people disagree about what global businesses mean for international relations and power, because they start from different assumptions about how the world works. Ruggie then asks the question all three perspectives have to answer: what kind of thing is a multinational, politically? Read in that order because they build on each other.

Alfaro 2016 — “Gains from Foreign Direct Investment”

Genre: descriptive empirical / literature survey.

Alfaro is not testing a hypothesis with her own data. She is summarizing what a large body of empirical research has found and arguing for how the field should proceed. Do not go looking for a hypothesis or a test of it.

What she is doing

The naive question is “does FDI benefit host countries?” Alfaro’s answer is that this question is badly posed. The gains are conditional — they depend on local conditions, policies, and institutions. She calls for an integrated approach that combines macro-level evidence (comparing countries) with micro-level evidence (firm-level data) plus theory, because neither level answers the question alone. You need to read this piece to understand more what the evidence says about these conditional effects.

Key concepts in this reading

  • Complementarities — FDI pays off in combination with something else already present in the host economy
  • Absorptive capacity — the host economy’s ability to actually capitalize on resources a foreign firm brings (skills, technology, know-how)
  • Spillovers — benefits that leak from the foreign firm to local firms
  • Selection vs. reallocation vs. spillover — three different reasons productivity might rise when multinationals arrive, which look identical in aggregate data but mean very different things

Note-taking focus: the conditions under which FDI pays off, and why measuring the benefit is harder than it sounds.

Gilpin 1976 — “The Political Economy of the Multinational Corporation”

Genre: review essay doing grand theory.

This one will confuse you if nobody warns you, so here is the warning. Gilpin is reviewing three books — Kari Levitt’s Silent Surrender, a volume edited by Jagdish Bhagwati, and Raymond Vernon’s Sovereignty at Bay. He is not summarizing them one at a time as a courtesy. He is using them as specimens: each book represents a whole worldview about how economics and politics relate, and by contrasting the three he builds a framework that has been influential to the discipline ever since.

You are not responsible for the three books. You are responsible for the three perspectives.

The three perspectives, and what they are called

Perspective Gilpin’s section heading The one-line version (you need to fill this in)
Liberalism “The Liberal Conception of the MNC”
Mercantilism / realism “The Mercantilist Conception of the MNC”
Marxism “The Marxist Conception of the MNC”

You may encounter these ideas elsewhere in this course, in others, or in your broader non-academic life under different names — “nationalist” for mercantilism/realism, “critical” for marxism. We use Gilpin’s terms because they match more closely to the preferred terminology in International Relations, and that’s the field in which I am trained.

How to read it

Gilpin lays out the three perspectives on two axes before he applies them: whether economic relations are fundamentally harmonious or conflictual, and whether economics or politics is primary. He includes a comparison table early on — find it, and use it as your map for the rest of the essay.

Then build this grid as you read. Filling it in is your notes for this reading:

Liberalism Mercantilism / realism Marxism
Are relations harmonious or conflictual?
Who are the real actors?
Is economics or politics primary?
What is the multinational?
Who benefits?
What happens to the nation-state?

The thing students miss

Gilpin is not neutral. Notice where he is describing a position and where he is judging it. You should have a sense of his position by the end of the article.

It is also worth reflecting on what parts of each perspective have held up in the 50 years since this was written, and what — if any — components of theory need to be modified to fit today’s realities.

Ruggie 2018 — “Multinationals as Global Institution”

Genre: conceptual/theoretical article with a contained argument.

This is closest to what you probably picture when you hear “scholarly article” — a clear question, a structured argument, a conclusion. But it is still conceptual rather than hypothesis-testing. Ruggie is not measuring anything. He is establishing what kind of entity a multinational is, using examples to illustrate rather than to test.

The structure — use it as a map

The article does exactly what its subtitle says, in order:

  1. Introduction — the debate he is intervening in (voluntary CSR vs. binding legal obligations)
  2. The multinational enterprise — what an MNE is, first economically, then legally
  3. Power — subdivided into instrumental, structural, and discursive
  4. Authority
  5. Relative autonomy
  6. Conclusion — what this means for the CSR-vs-treaty debate

Sections 3, 4, and 5 are the conceptual core of his analysis and the components on which you should especially focus (his point about the legal structure of MNEs is also very good to know). Section 1 and the parts of Section 6 about treaty design are not directly relevant to what we’ll discuss in class on Wednesday, but they will become increasingly relevant as the semester progresses. So, it is okay to read these sections lightly and to remember that it might be worth returning to this article after the midterm.

The three core concepts and the three subconcepts nested in the first

Power is relational — A getting B to do something B would not otherwise do. Ruggie splits it three ways, and you need to be able to define and differentiate all three:

  • Instrumental — deploying specific resources.
  • Structural — getting favorable outcomes without having to act, because of the position you occupy.
  • Discursive — shaping the ideas and norms that everyone takes for granted

Authority is not just more power. The difference is the voluntary suspension of individual judgment — the belief that the entity has a right to prescribe. Where does Ruggie think corporate authority comes from?

Relative autonomy is about the extent to which multinationals exercise authority on behalf of themselves, their owners, or another actor.

Note-taking focus: definitions of the three concepts in your own words, one concrete example each, plus why the fact that multinationals are legally separate from each other generates major implications for their actions and the ability of governments to exert authority over them.

Putting the readings in conversation

I pick every reading I assign to you for a specific reason. We should always consider how the readings relate to each other. Alfaro treats multinationals as economic actors whose effects we can measure. Gilpin and Ruggie treat them as political actors. Which framing is (more) right — and does it depend on what you are trying to explain?
