How to Read Scholarly Work

Scholarly articles are not textbooks, and they are not written primarily for students. Instead, they are written by scholars to transmit scholarly ideas for critique and debate to other scholars. Accordingly, you need to approach college reading — and especially scholarly articles — differently than other forms of writing. This is a skill, rather than a gift, and so it can be learned. This guide provides you with a structure or method for how to approach a variety of types of scholarly materials in a way to efficiently and effectively extract meaning from them. Most of the time, faculty expect students to learn this skill over time through trial and error, and indeed, to become proficient at reading scholarly material, you need to practice (see Ted and Lizzie, but not Allen). But, hopefully this guide helps to unmask the “hidden curriculum” of college.

The bottom line up front (the BLUF, in State Department Speak)

A textbook chapter is organized to deliver information, usually communicated as settled fact. A scholarly article is organized to pose a question or puzzle; propose a solution, answer, or conceptual framework to interpreting the question; and to defend this proposition.

That difference changes how you read scholarly work as opposed to a textbook. You are not trying to absorb everything on the page. You are trying to find four elements:

  1. The question the author is asking
  2. The answer they give (their claim)
  3. The reason the answer is supposed to be true (their mechanism or evidence)
  4. The limits of their claim (does the evidence support it, does the mechanism make sense, are there alternative explanations for the empirical evidence, are there scope conditions that limit how broadly their answer applies, etc.)

If you complete a reading with those four components written down in your own words, you have done your job. Everything else in the article exists to support them.

The three-pass method

Do not read a scholarly article front-to-back like a novel. Read it three times, fast to slow. This takes less total time than one grinding read-through, because by the time you read carefully you already know what matters. (Note that the cases you are reading in this course are an entirely different genre of writing, and your approach to those should be different. We will discuss separately.)

Pass 1 — Orient (5–10 minutes)

Read only: title, abstract, introduction, every section heading, and the conclusion. Skip the whole middle.

Then, before going further, answer in one sentence each:

  • What is this about?
  • What does the author want me to believe by the end?

If you cannot answer these yet, read the introduction again. Authors almost always state their claim in the last paragraph or two of the introduction — look for phrases like “I argue that,” “this article shows,” or “the central claim is.”

Pass 2 — Read for meat of the argument or conceptual contribution (time depends on the article length and complexity)

Now read it through. But read like you are following an argument, not collecting facts.

  • Read the first sentence of each paragraph. Academic paragraphs are usually built so the first sentence carries the point. If that sentence tells you what you need, move on.
  • Chase the “because.” Every claim should have a “because” to defend it. Find and mark the because. If the because is missing, that’s a weakness of the article that you should note.
  • Let go of the details you do not need. You are allowed to skim a statistical table, a long literature review, or three pages of an example you already understand. Skimming on purpose is a skill, not a failure. Just don’t overdo it, especially when you are reading material on a topic that is completely new to you.
  • Look up unfamiliar terms sparingly. Any more and you are researching, not reading. Guess from context and keep moving; often the author defines it two pages later anyway. Keep a list of unfamiliar terms that you can look up later or ask me in class to define.

Pass 3 — Interrogate (5–10 minutes)

Close the article. Then ask:

  • Do I actually buy this? What would have to be true for it to hold?
  • What is the strongest objection someone could raise?
  • What does this let me see that I could not see before?
  • Where does it connect to something else in this course?

Pass 3 is where the reading becomes your tool for analytic discovery rather than just a random thing you’ve happened to read. Students who spend the time to interrogate the article before class can contribute more meaningfully to discussion (even if the contribution is asking a very incisive question rather than confidently asserting a claim). Such students can also write more fluently about the readings and connect them to our cases.

Know what kind of material you are reading, and adjust accordingly

“Scholarly article” covers several very different genres. The four questions generalize across scholarly articles, but where you find the answers changes depending on genre. Figure out the genre in Pass 1 and you will stop looking for things in the wrong places.

Genre How to recognize it Argument location On what to take notes
Descriptive empirical / survey Reports what a body of research has found; lots of citations to other studies, few or no original tables Intro and conclusion — the author’s claim is about the state of knowledge The pattern in the findings, the conditions under which it holds, what is still unsettled
Hypothesis-testing empirical Has original data, a method section, results tables, a stated hypothesis Intro states the finding; results section tests it The hypothesis, how the concepts were measured, what result would have falsified it
Theory / conceptual article No data; defines and distinguishes concepts Distributed across the piece; the conclusion pulls it together The definitions, the distinctions, the mechanism connecting them
Review essay Discusses several other people’s books or articles The author’s own take on how the works relate — often a framework of their own Each position, and what the reviewer thinks each gets right or wrong
Case study Narrative about one firm, country, or event Often implicit — the author shows rather than tells Decision-maker, decision, constraints, evidence, outcome
Policy report Institutional author (UN, World Bank, think tank), executive summary Executive summary and recommendations The recommendation and the interest behind it

Two genre traps to remember

“This author just keeps describing other people’s work.” You may be holding a review essay or a literature survey. The author is not confused and has not become lost in their writing — mapping the debate is the contribution. Read for the map of positions, not for a single buried thesis. And watch for the analytic move where a reviewer uses other people’s work to build a framework of their own; that framework is usually the real payoff.

“There is data here but no hypothesis.” Not all empirical work tests a hypothesis. Descriptive empirical work establishes what is true — patterns, magnitudes, conditions — and that is a legitimate and demanding contribution in its own right. Do not go hunting for a hypothesis test that was never promised. Later in the course you will read hypothesis-testing work, and you will read it differently.

A note-taking template that works for all scholarly writing

Use this for every non-case reading. It might only be half a page, handwritten or typed. It should take five minutes after Pass 2, and you should save this material in your course folder.

Citation: author, year, short title

Genre: empirical / conceptual / review / case / policy

Question: what is the author trying to explain or settle?

Claim: their answer, in your words — one or two sentences, no quoting

Mechanism or evidence: why is the claim supposed to hold? What is doing the work?

Key concepts: the two or three terms you would need to use this reading in an argument

Scope and limits: when would this stop being true?

My pushback: one objection, question, or thing that did not convince you

Course link: which case, concept, or session does this speak to?

Two extremely important rules to increase effectiveness:

Write the claim in your own words. If you can only copy the author’s sentence, you have not understood it yet. (This also means to not use AI because your notes are not summaries, they are analysis. You need to do them yourself.)

Always fill in “my pushback.” Even a weak objection forces you into an active stance toward the text. “I did not understand why X follows from Y” is a perfectly good entry — and it is often the best thing you can bring to class.

Annotating without drowning in yellow

If you highlight everything, you have highlighted nothing. Try marking in the margin instead, with a small code:

  • C — the central claim
  • ? — I do not follow this
  • ! — this surprised me or I disagree
  • → — connects to something else we read
  • def — a definition I will need later

The point of margin marks is that you can find your way back.

When you get lost

You will get lost. Everyone does, including me (especially if the writing is poor). What separates strong readers is how they adjust:

  • Keep going. Confusion in the middle often resolves by the conclusion. Read to the end before you decide you are stuck.
  • Go back to the introduction. Nine times out of ten, the thing you lost was the thread of the argument, and the intro is where the argument is mostly clearly articulated.
  • Name the confusion specifically. “I do not get this article” is not usable. “I do not see how his definition of authority differs from power” is a question you can bring to class — and it is usually a question several other people also have.
  • Do not assume the fault is yours. Sometimes an argument really is underspecified, and noticing that is an important skill.

How much is enough?

You have read well enough when you can, without looking at the page:

  • State the author’s claim in one sentence
  • Say why they think it is true
  • Name one thing that would make you doubt it
  • Say what it has to do with this course

Reading in this course specifically

The Resources page explains how our two kinds of reading do different jobs. Briefly:

  • Monday (conceptual readings) give you an analytic framework and rigorous grounding in the core concept we’re covering for the week. Read Monday material for the argument, the mechanism, and the evidence. Your notes should end up mostly in the “claim,” “mechanism,” and “key concepts” lines of the template.
  • Wednesday (cases) are not scholarly readings. Instead, they are curated stories about a problem related to global business that allows us to apply the theories and concepts from Monday to specific examples, with an emphasis on how we would make decisions if we were in charge. Read actively for the key facts of the scenario, the decision-maker, the potential decision they could make, the constraints, and the evidence. Come ready to use the case, not just recall it. Pay special attention for connections between the case and core concept for the week.
