Syllabus

INTL-I103: Global Business — Politics, Policy & Practice · Fall 2026

Course at a glance

Instructor: Prof. Sarah Bauerle Danzman · sbauerle@indiana.edu Meeting time: Mondays & Wednesdays, 9:35–10:50 AM Location: GISB 1112 Term: Aug 24 – Dec 9, 2026 Office hours: M/W 2-3:30 PM (GISB 1013), and by appointment

This syllabus is a living document. The schedule and assigned readings are subject to change; the most current version always lives on this site.

Course description

What does it mean to be a global corporation in the 21st century? What dilemmas do business leaders face operating in countries with varying political, cultural, technological, and environmental landscapes? Who holds power in the global economy and how does this affect corporate behavior? How can communities harness the positive aspects of global business while managing the social, environmental, and security risks they generate?

In this course you will analyze the opportunities and challenges multinational companies generate using real cases from global companies including Nike, ExxonMobil, Siemens, Philip Morris, Apple, Patagonia, H&M, TikTok, and Fuyao (American Factory). Individually and in teams, you will use political, economic, historical, and cultural knowledge to critically analyze, debate, and solve real-world problems business, political, and community leaders face — building skills in risk analysis, ethical reasoning, and cultural competence.

Aims & outcomes

By the end of this course you will be able to describe, explain, and propose solutions for the problems multinational enterprises (MNEs) confront and create in international contexts. Specifically, you will be able to:

  • Describe the global production structure, the history of the MNE, and the major controversies surrounding their operations.
  • Explain and assess major IR approaches to identifying sources of power in the global economy and how actors translate power into influence and authority.
  • Critically assess the political, institutional, and cultural roots of an MNE’s operational challenges.
  • Evaluate MNEs’ environmental, social, and governance impact on the communities where they operate.
  • Assess when “responsible business conduct” policies such as CSR work — and how political and economic structures can undermine them.
  • Identify the national-security and economic-statecraft tools states use to manage MNE activity — tariffs, investment screening, export controls — and assess the tradeoffs involved.

You will develop transferable analytic skills as you assess the concrete problems MNEs create through varied evidence, diagnose their root causes, and propose solutions. These aims fulfill International Studies Department Learning Outcomes in Global Knowledge, Global Skills, and Global Thinking.

How this course works

This is an in-person, case-based course that meets twice a week with a deliberate rhythm:

Mondays — framing

Reading discussion, lecture, and concepts. We build the week’s ideas and set up the case.

Wednesdays — in-class group case write-ups

You work with your team during class to analyze the week’s case and produce a written deliverable, turned in before the end of the period. Reading the prompt, discussing, drafting, and submitting all happen in the room that day — there’s no take-home component and nothing is due the following Monday. Because this work happens in class, attendance on Wednesdays is essential.

Grading philosophy

Grades are necessary but imperfect assessments of achievement at a moment in time — not a judgment of your worth, character, or potential. I do not entertain requests to “round up” or for ad-hoc extra credit. At the end of the term, if you are within 0.5% of the next higher letter grade and have turned in all assignments on time, I will round up; if you have not been timely, I will not. If you email asking for a boost, I will not provide one. If you’re concerned about your grade, come to office hours early — the period between the last class and final grade submission is not an appropriate time to discuss grades, except for a potential grading error.

Course policies

Intellectual diversity

This course examines the intersection of politics, policy, and global business, with materials presenting a variety of arguments about the normative and economic value of global business. Connections to current and historical events will come up; analyzing connections among ideas and events is a core liberal-arts skill. The course is designed for thoughtful, reasoned debate. Four rules help:

  1. Frame disagreements as debates rather than conflicts.
  2. Argue as if you are right, but listen as if you are wrong.
  3. Make the most respectful interpretation of the other person’s perspective.
  4. Acknowledge where you agree with others and what you’ve learned from peers with whom you differ.

Generative AI policy

The central writing in this course — the group case write-ups — is completed in class, by hand or on a shared document, during our Wednesday meetings, and turned in before class ends. There is no take-home draft or revision stage, by design: keeping the entire write-up inside the class period is how this course limits generative AI use. You may not use generative AI tools to produce this work; the value is in the analytic effort you and your teammates do together in the room. For exam preparation and reading, likewise develop your own ability to read critically and reason without outsourcing that thinking. If you’re ever unsure whether a particular use of an AI tool is permitted, ask first. Unpermitted use, or use without appropriate attribution, violates the IU Code of Student Rights, Responsibilities, and Conduct.

Accommodations for religious observances

IU respects the right of all students to observe their religion. If you require accommodations for a religious observance, submit a request by the end of the second week (a separate request per absence). I will approve these provided they’re filed within the first two weeks, and you’ll have an opportunity to make up missed work.

Email & office hours

Reserve email for clarifying questions or scheduling; office hours are for substantive questions and grade discussions. I try to respond within one business day. Office hours are also a chance for me to get to know you — which works to your advantage (I write much better recommendation letters for students I know). Arrive with specific questions.

Re-grading requests

After graded material is returned, I maintain a 48-hour reflection period before discussing grades. When I re-examine work with fresh eyes, I reserve the right to assign a grade lower than the original if appropriate.

Honor code

As an IU student you are expected to adhere to the Code of Student Rights, Responsibilities, and Conduct. Submitting work with your name on it signifies it is yours unless otherwise cited. Suspected violations are handled per University policy and reported to the Dean of Students.
