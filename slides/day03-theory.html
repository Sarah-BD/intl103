What is a global business?

Recall from Day 1: MNEs are the global economy

  • MNEs grew from ~7,000 (1970) to over 100,000 (2011), with ~900,000 foreign affiliates

  • By 2016, MNEs and their affiliates accounted for ~36% of global output, ⅔ of exports, and ½ of imports

Definitions

Term What it means
Foreign portfolio investment Cross-border purchase of stocks or bonds purely for a financial return, with no intent to control.
Multinational enterprise (MNE) A firm that owns and controls production facilities or other income-generating assets in at least two countries. You’ll also see MNC and TNC — same class of entities, slightly different terminology.
Foreign direct investment (FDI) Cross-border investment that gives a lasting interest and a measure of control — by convention, a stake of 10% or more in a foreign enterprise.
Parent & affiliate The home-country parent owns the foreign affiliate — the umbrella term for any foreign operation in which the parent holds a lasting stake. FDI is the financial flow that builds and sustains that relationship.
Subsidiary An affiliate the parent majority-owns (>50%) — legally a separate company, but under the parent’s control.
Home Country The “nationality,” primary place of business, headquarters, or place of origination of the MNE. How to define home country is contested.
Host Country The country receiving the investment.

The decisive word is control. Buy 5% of Toyota stock for the dividend → portfolio investment. Buy or build a plant you run → direct investment. Control is what turns a flow of money into a multinational firm.

Going global: how and why

The ladder of going global

Firms don’t flip a switch from “domestic” to “multinational.” They climb a ladder of rising commitment, cost, and control.

Non-equity modes (export, license, franchise, subcontract) are “asset-lite” — quicker and less costly, but they cede control.

Equity modes (FDI) — joint venture, greenfield, M&A — are costlier and slower, but the firm owns and operates the activity.

Going global isn’t binary; it’s a ladder, and the rungs are ordered by how much commitment, cost, and control each involves. At the bottom, the cheapest and most reversible option is simply to export. One step up, non-equity arrangements let a firm earn from a foreign market without owning anything there: licensing (rent out your IP), franchising (rent out your brand and business model under strict conditions, the way McDonald’s or Marriott expand), and long-term subcontracting. Quick, inexpensive, low-risk — but the firm surrenders control over how the product is made and who captures the value. Then the firm crosses a real threshold into FDI, the equity modes, where it takes an ownership stake and with it managerial control. A joint venture shares equity and risk with a local partner. A wholly-owned subsidiary is full commitment, in two forms that matter for host countries: greenfield (new capacity, new jobs) and M&A (historically the larger share of FDI by value — transfers ownership of assets that already exist). Every step up buys more control and more of the value, but costs more, moves slower, and exposes the firm to more risk.

Why do firms go global?

  • Resource-seeking — raw materials, labor, inputs
  • Market-seeking — sell into a new market
  • Efficiency-seeking — lower costs by relocating
  • Export platforming — produce in one place to export elsewhere
  • Strategic asset-seeking - acquire strategically beneficial brands, production capabilities, or technologies

When asked, most investors say markets.

Source: World Bank, Global Investment Competitiveness survey

Two ways to structure FDI

Horizontal FDI — replicate production in another country to capture that market or for regulatory arbitrage

  • Many services require local presence

Vertical FDI — fragment the value chain across locations, placing each stage where it’s cheapest or most advantageous

  • This is what built global value chains — and it’s why the trade network thickened so dramatically in a generation →

Source: World Bank, World Development Report 2020

Why pick one entry strategy over another?

FDI is costly due to the “liability of foreignness” — less understanding of local consumer tastes, politics, institutions, language, culture. So firms prefer asset-lite strategies unless a market friction makes control preferable:

  • Ownership — firm-specific assets you don’t want to share
  • Location — cost of production, local resources, proximity to markets
  • Internalization — keep the asset in-house rather than contract it out (high transaction costs, market imperfections, quality control)
No horizontal FDI Horizontal FDI
Vertical FDI Hyper-efficient, specialized, export platform (semiconductors) Market + Efficiency-seeking; global brands/supply chains (autos)
No vertical FDI Unlikely to be global firm Market-seeking; global service brands

Who benefits from FDI?

Why countries seek investment

  • Capital investment is necessary to raise productivity — and productivity growth has a positive effect on incomes
  • Poor countries are often savings-constrained: domestic savings can’t finance the investment they need
  • Foreign capital can fill the gap — which is why so many governments pursue it

Source:

The case for FDI

  • FDI transfers technology and know-how, raising output and value-added in the host economy
  • It creates jobs — and can generate positive spillovers to local firms and workers
  • Economies that attract FDI become more central in global value chains — compounding their access to technology, demand, and upgrading opportunities

Source: World Bank, World Development Report 2020

Domestic firms can better integrate into the most lucrative parts of global supply chains by linking up with global firms. People who work for MNEs tend to have higher wages, because MNEs are more productive and more technology intensive than local firms. Companies that go global also tend to expand domestically as well, and create jobs both at home and abroad.

The critique

  • Critics argue global firms keep most of the profit in the home country (repatriation — especially natural-resource MNEs)
  • The host state might become structurally dependent on MNEs
  • MNEs might displace local business, impeding indigenous development — particularly around technology (patents and rents)
  • MNEs might feel little need to cultivate lasting relationships with local actors (enclave FDI)

Global footprints mean a restructuring of what kinds of activities are undertaken where, which has distributive effects both in terms of people and in terms of countries. When Apple subcontracts its assembly overseas, Americans who would have had those jobs are likely unable to reskill into R&D jobs (or move to CA). And the countries to which such tasks moved — and the people who perform those tasks — keep a very small piece of the pie of value added.

These worries have deep historical roots

  • Pre-WWI: investment mostly through banking and portfolio flows, structured by colonial ties — mostly transportation and infrastructure
  • 1920s–1970s: decolonization, economic nationalism, and statism → widespread FDI restrictions and expropriation of foreign assets
  • 1980s–2015: neoliberalism, privatization, and transitions to market economies → openness and active FDI attraction

Key facts: most FDI flows among developed countries (largely via M&A); developing countries moved from resource-extraction FDI to manufacturing to services; the poorest countries are almost entirely excluded from the FDI network.

Alfaro: “Does FDI help?” is the wrong question

Alfaro (2016) surveys the empirical literature — and the macro evidence shows no automatic growth payoff from FDI on its own.

The gains are conditional on complementarities — what’s already present in the host economy:

  • Human capital — FDI promotes growth only above a minimum education threshold (Borensztein et al. 1998)
  • Local financial markets — FDI × financial development is what’s positive and significant, not FDI alone (Alfaro et al. 2004)
  • Policy environment, institutions, and market structure condition the rest

Her term for all this: absorptive capacity — the host economy’s ability to actually capitalize on what foreign firms bring.

Alfaro: three reasons productivity rises when MNEs arrive

Mechanism What’s happening Do locals gain?
Spillover Knowledge leaks from foreign to domestic firms Yes — local firms improve
Selection Least productive domestic firms exit Mixed — survivors are stronger, but firms die
Reallocation Resources shift from domestic to multinational firms Not necessarily — averages rise without local firms improving

All three look identical in aggregate data — and the policy stakes are big: if spillovers drive the gains, tax breaks for foreign firms may be justified. If it’s mostly reallocation, that money is better spent on local conditions — credit access, labor supply, fewer regulatory barriers.

What the evidence shows

  • FDI is associated with growth — but not automatically; spillovers depend on absorptive capacity, so the very poorest countries struggle to reap the benefits
  • FDI jobs pay wage premiums over local firms as high as ~30% (Lipsey and Sjöholm 2004)
  • FDI inflows from OECD countries are associated with stronger collective labor rights (Mosley and Uno 2007)
  • Local firms can integrate into MNE supply chains — but it’s context-specific
  • Alfaro’s bottom line: the best policies improve local conditions — which both attracts FDI and maximizes what the host gets from it

Three ways of seeing

Gilpin: three worldviews, not three book reports

Gilpin (1976) reviews three books — but he’s really using them as specimens of three perspectives on the multinational corporation.

Each perspective answers two prior questions differently:

  • Are international economic relations fundamentally harmonious or conflictual?
  • Is economics or politics in the driver’s seat?

Answer those two questions and the rest of each worldview — including who benefits — follows.

Gilpin’s three perspectives

Liberalism Mercantilism / realism Marxism
Key actors Firms States Classes
Primary goal of economic activity Generate wealth Maximize state power Entrench capitalist elite
Nature of interaction Cooperation among economic actors Conflict between states Conflict between rich & poor
Implications State should avoid intervening in markets; this creates most wealth States should shape markets to build power, even if that means forgoing wealth maximization Capitalists have captured the state and collude to build their wealth at expense of workers

Key takeaways

  • A global business is defined by control across borders — and firms weigh cost, time horizon, and control in choosing how to go global
  • FDI happens when a market friction makes control worth the liability of foreignness
  • FDI’s benefits to host countries are real but conditional — on absorptive capacity and local complementarities, not automatic (Alfaro)
  • Liberalism, mercantilism/realism, and marxism disagree about who benefits — keep all three lenses; different cases will reward different ones
